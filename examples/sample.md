# I. The Tenant at Wuthering Hall

## The Journal of Mr. Lockwood

I have just completed a transaction that, in the whole of my history as a
clerk, has no parallel. My employer, Mr. Jonathan Prynne of the firm Prynne,
Hale, and Son, solicitors to His Majesty's dead and dying, entrusted me with a
matter of singular delicacy: the conveyance of a remote Yorkshire property
called Wuthering Hall to its sole remaining legatee, a Miss Catherine Linton,
of whom nothing was known save that she had not answered a letter in seven
years.

You will think it strange that a clerk of respectable habits should accept a
journey of two hundred miles into the moor country for the settling of a
lease. But the name had worked upon my imagination as few things do. Wuthering
Hall. The very sound of it conjured for me a house of black stone crouched
against the wind, its windows like the eyes of something that had learned to
wait. And I am, though I pretend otherwise, a man who reads novels after
midnight.

I arrived at the railway terminus of Gimmerton under a sky the colour of an
old bruise. The stationmaster, a rheumatic fellow with a coal-dust
cheek, regarded my portmanteau with the interest of a man who has not seen
three travellers in a season. When I named my destination, his face underwent
a curious tightening.

"Ay," he said, drawing the word out like a thread from a seam. "Th' Hall.
'Tis a melancholy place, if ye ask me. Though they do say the doctor from
Grimhaven keeps a light in the west tower that never goes out, winter or
summer, and that a coach can be seen some nights climbing the fell road
though no coach is owned within ten miles."

"A superstition," I said, with the confidence of a man who has never spent a
night on a moor.

"Ay," he agreed, which is the Yorkshireman's way of telling you that you are a
fool and that he will watch you find it out for yourself.

## The House

I engaged a trap and drove out along the fell road as the light failed. The
moor was a great dark animal breathing under the dusk, and the road ran over
its flank like a scar. The wind, which had been merely unpleasant at the
station, gathered itself upon the open ground and struck the little cart with
a force that made the driver curse and lean into his reins. Heather bent flat
before us. Somewhere beyond the shoulder of the fell, I heard a sound I took
at first for the cry of a bird, and then did not take for a bird at all. It
was a voice, thin and high, calling a name into the wind — but the driver
cracked his whip and I did not ask, and he did not offer.

The Hall revealed itself gradually, as such houses do, so that it seemed to
grow out of the stone rather than to stand upon it. It was a long, low
building of gritstone, two storeys high, with a projecting tower at one end
whose upper windows were dark. Small, deep-set casements, narrow as arrow
slits, gave the facade the aspect of a face that has turned its attention
inward and does not mean to be disturbed. A grove of wind-bent sycamores
leaned away from it as if in perpetual flight. At the top of the tower, true
to the stationmaster's word, a single point of light burned: a lamp, and
behind it, I could not help but fancy, a figure.

The door was opened, not by a servant, but by a woman of perhaps sixty, grey
as the house itself, in a black gown of an old-fashioned cut. She regarded me
with eyes that had seen too much and had decided, long ago, not to be
impressed by anything further.

"Mr. Lockwood," she said. It was not a question.

"Mrs. Dean, I presume? Miss Linton's housekeeper?"

"I have kept this house for forty years," she said. "And kept its secrets for
longer. Come in out of the wind. The master is expected, though not by me."

I followed her through a low, flagged hall hung with oil paintings so darkened
by smoke that their subjects had become mere suggestions of faces. My heels
echoed on the stone. Somewhere above us, something heavy was being dragged —
or was being moved, which is not the same thing — across a floor, very slowly,
and then the sound stopped, as if the one who dragged had heard me listening.

"The west tower," said Mrs. Dean, without turning. "That will be Dr. Creed.
He keeps the lamp. He keeps many things, Mr. Lockwood. This house is a
collection of the things Dr. Creed keeps."

"Miss Linton," I said, "does she live here alone, then?"

Mrs. Dean stopped. When she spoke again, her voice had lost the iron of the
Yorkshire and gone soft, like a hand opening.

"There is a story they tell on the fell," she said. "That a house can come to
house the dead if the living will not do their part. Wuthering Hall is not
haunted, Mr. Lockwood. It is inhabited. There is a difference, and it is the
difference between a grave that is empty and a grave that is only waiting."

She left me at the door of a parlour lit by a single lamp, where a fire had
been laid that had not yet been lit. And that was where I met Catherine.

## Miss Linton

She was seated in a high-backed chair by the cold hearth, a book open face-down
upon her knee, as if she had put it aside only an instant before, although
the parlour had been empty. I had formed, from the solicitor's file, an idea
of a withered spinster of unremarkable age. Catherine Linton was perhaps
twenty-five. Her hair, the colour of the moor's own heather in late summer,
was caught back loosely; her eyes were grey and large and had in them a
watchfulness that made me feel, within seconds of entering, that I was the
one being examined. She did not rise.

"Mr. Lockwood," she said. "You have come a long way to give me a house."

"I have come," I said, "to give you a signature, and the house comes with it.
Mr. Prynne sends his respects, and wishes to know if you have reconsidered his
earlier letter."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, very quietly, "You should
not have come at night."

"The train —"

"The nights here are not like the nights elsewhere, Mr. Lockwood. On the fell,
night is not the absence of day. It is a thing with its own shape and its own
appetites. My father used to say that the moors were not a place you passed
through, but a thing that passed through you."

I attempted a smile. "Your father was evidently a poet as well as a
landowner."

"He was a physician," she said, "and he is dead these three years. You will
meet what remains of his work in the west tower, if you stay long enough.
Everyone who stays long enough meets it."

There was a quality in the room then that I can only describe as a lowering of
the air, as before a storm. The lamp flame, which had burned steadily, bent
and stood again. Catherine's eyes went past me, to the door, and I turned,
expecting to see the figure of Mrs. Dean. The doorway was empty. But I felt,
with an intensity that shames me to record, the pressure of a gaze that was
not mine.

"If you mean to settle the papers," Catherine said, "you will have your
answer by morning. I will not decide anything tonight. I never decide anything
after dark. There are decisions that should not be taken in the dark, Mr.
Lockwood. My husband taught me that."

"Your husband?"

The grey eyes held mine. "My husband is dead," she said. "I have been a widow
for seven years. And I have received, every night since he died, at midnight,
a letter from him. Mrs. Dean thinks it is the wind in the chimneys."

"And what do you think it is?"

"I think," said Catherine Linton, "that the wind is very good at pretending to
be my husband. But the wind does not know the things he knows."

Outside, the moor wind gathered itself and struck the house like a hand. The
lamp flame bent, held, and died. In the dark that followed, I heard her rise,
and her skirts, and the door closing softly; and I stood alone in the
blackness of Wuthering Hall, with the cold of the fell coming up through the
flags, and the knowledge that I had come to give a dead woman's house to a
woman who was waiting for the dead.

I did not sleep. And at midnight, in the wind, I heard what she had heard: a
voice, thin and persistent, calling through the chimney, over and over, a
name.

It was not mine. And yet I found I was answering.

# II. The Physician's Case-Log

## Extracted from the Records of Dr. Erasmus Creed

Dr. Creed keeps, in the west tower, what he calls his Case-Log: a ledger of
elephant folio, bound in leather black as dried blood, in which he has
recorded, in a hand that grows stranger the further one reads, the progress of
his chief patient. Mrs. Dean permitted me to examine the volume upon the
second morning, when the wind had fallen and Catherine had gone out upon the
fell, and the house lay about me in that particular hush that follows a
storm's departure, as if it were catching its breath.

I transcribe here, for the edification of the reader and the confusion of the
medical profession, selected entries. The doctor's own headings are preserved.

## Entries

*1 November.* The subject of my study grows stronger. This morning he lifted,
with one hand, the iron cot in which I had believed him confined, and set it
down again with the delicacy of a man replacing a teacup upon its saucer. He
does not speak of strength. He speaks of loneliness, which he pronounces as a
man might pronounce the name of a disease he has been told is incurable. He
asked again after his maker. I have told him that his maker is dead. I have
told him this many times. He listens each time with the same terrible
courtesy, and then asks again.

*5 November.* Miss Linton visited the tower for the first time in a year. She
did not look at the subject. She looked at me, and said, "He should not have
been made." I replied that all things that are made were made by someone, and
that the responsibility lies with the maker, a position of orthodox
theology. She said, "Then we are in agreement. And the maker is you." She has
inherited her father's habit of being right about things one would rather she
were wrong about.

*7 November.* At midnight, a knock upon the tower door. No one was there; the
wind had simply, it seemed, delivered a letter, which lay upon the threshold.
It bore no stamp, and the hand upon the envelope I did not know. It was sealed
with a crest I have not seen in twenty years: a tower, and beneath it, a wolf.
I have not opened it. I have placed it in the drawer with the other letters
that I have not opened, and I have locked the drawer, and I have burned the
key, and still, when I close my eyes, I can see the crest as clearly as if it
were pressed against the inside of my eyelids. The wind is a very good
postman, Mr. Lockwood. It delivers things that should not have been sent.

*10 November.* The subject spoke of the sea. He has never seen the sea. He
described it with such exactness that I checked the atlas; his description
matched the Baltic coast to the mile. He says he dreams of it, and that in the
dreams he is standing at the rail of a ship, and that the ship is carrying
him somewhere he has been before, to a land of wolves and snow. He says that
the dreams feel like memory, and that he does not know whose memory they are.
I have begun, against my better judgement, to record his dreams. The record
grows.

*12 November.* I made the journey into Grimhaven for laudanum and found
instead a parcel upon my own desk, though no servant in the house admits to
having seen it delivered. It contained a single sheet, upon which was drawn,
in ink of a red I have not seen in nature, a map of the house. The west tower
was marked. Beneath it was written, in a hand that trembled in the writing
though the intention was firm: *the maker will be asked to answer for the
making.* I have burned the sheet. The fear it occasioned, I have not burned,
for the excellent reason that fear, being immaterial, declines to burn.

*13 November.* The subject wept tonight. It is the first time I have seen it,
and it was more terrible than the strength. He wept, not for himself, but for
the companion he says his maker owed him, the bride who was promised in the
writing of the design and withheld in the hour of completion. "The pen
promised her," he said. "The hand wrote her. The hand is you." I had no answer.
I have no answer. The pen and the hand and the maker are, as he correctly
observes, all mine.

*15 November.* Tonight the subject asked to see the moon. I had thought the
tower windows too high; he had thought otherwise, and had contrived, out of
objects that a lesser mind would have called a bedframe and a bolster, a
mechanism whose purpose I did not grasp until it had been accomplished, and the
shutters were open, and the moon stood in the west tower window like a guest
who has forgotten his manners. He looked at it a long time. Then he said,
"Where I am going, there is no moon." I asked where he was going. He said, "To
the sea. To the ship. To the land of wolves. The maker will not keep me here
for long. I have been patient, but patience is a house that can only hold one
tenant." I have begun, this night, to believe him.

*17 November.* Miss Linton came to the tower at dusk. She would not come at
night. She asked me if I believed in the soul. I answered, as physicians do,
that the question was not in my remit. She said that the question was in mine,
because I had made the only proof of it she would ever require, and that the
proof was waiting behind the door of the west tower, and that it was patient,
and that it was lonely, and that I had taught it, by the very science of its
making, what it would cost to be denied. She said, "My husband told me once
that every man who makes a thing makes it in his own image. Look at it, Dr.
Creed, and tell me whose image it is."

I looked at the subject, who was watching the moon. It is not my image. It is
not the image of any man I know. And yet it is the image of something that
loves, and is not loved, and knows it, and that is an image I recognize
because I see it in the glass of the tower window every night when the lamp is
out.

I have taken to locking the door of the tower from the inside. It is a small
precaution. I do not flatter myself that it will hold.

## Postscript, in a later hand

The entries above are transcribed from the ledger of Dr. Erasmus Creed,
physician of Wuthering Hall, found after his disappearance in the second week
of December. The west tower lamp, which had burned without interruption for
forty years, was discovered extinguished on the morning of the 14th. The
doctor's instruments were in order. His coat lay folded upon his chair. The
bed of the subject was empty, and had been empty for some time, and upon the
pillow lay a single white hair, and beneath the hair, a note in a hand that
was not the doctor's, and not the housekeeper's, and not, so far as anyone
could say, entirely human:

> *The maker has gone to the sea, to answer for the making. I go where the
> moon does not. We will meet, all three of us, in the winter, upon the ice,
> where the promises of the pen are finally kept. — Adam*

# III. The Master of Carfax Castle

## A Letter, Delivered by Hand

I found the letter upon my pillow on the third morning, though the house had
been locked and Mrs. Dean swore to having seen no one. It was written on paper
so heavy it felt like vellum, and sealed with black wax bearing a crest I
confess I had already learned to dread: a tower, and beneath it, a wolf.

> *To Mr. Gabriel Lockwood, at Wuthering Hall, greetings.*
>
> *You will have heard my name spoken in this house in the manner in which one
> names a plague that has been contained. I am told you are a man of law, and
> therefore of accuracy, and therefore of use. I write to correct a legal
> error before it is committed, for I have some regard for the forms, and a
> will is a form, and a signature is a form, and the dead, my dear clerk, are
> the most punctilious of all litigants.*
>
> *Wuthering Hall is not Catherine's. It was never Catherine's. It was built by
> a foundling who had no name and took the name of the house that would not
> have him, and it passes, by rights older than your king, to the blood that
> paid for it in the manner that blood is truly paid. I enclose no documents.
> The documents you will find persuasive are inscribed elsewhere — in the
> ledger of a physician who has lately left his post, and in the memory of a
> housekeeper who is better at keeping than at speaking.*
>
> *You are advised to leave the fell before the winter closes it. The winter
> here is not the winter of your southern counties. It is a season with a
> memory, and the memory is long, and I am the memory of it.*
>
> *I will call upon the Hall at the hour that suits me. I do not knock. I am
> received.*
>
> *Yours, in the old style,*
> *D.*

I read the letter three times, and the third time I felt, with a clarity that
the daylight had not afforded, the weight of its central assertion. A foundling
who had no name and took the name of the house that would not have him. It
chimed, unpleasantly, with a story Mrs. Dean had begun to tell me at the
hearth the previous night, and had broken off when she saw Catherine
listening.

I went in search of the housekeeper and found her, as I might have guessed,
in the kitchen, beating a bread dough with a violence that suggested the dough
had offered an opinion.

"Mrs. Dean," I said, "who is D.?"

The beating paused. "That would be Lord Dorian Draven, of Carfax Castle,
beyond the fell. If it was written by D., which it was not, because the D. who
writes letters does not write letters, he has a messenger."

"The wind delivers his letters, according to Dr. Creed's ledger."

"The wind," said Mrs. Dean, with the air of a woman conceding a technical
point, "is one of his messengers."

## The History of the Foundling

She told me the story as the dough rose and the afternoon darkened, in the
flat, unornamented Yorkshire voice that she used when she was telling the
truth, which was not always, and never by accident.

Sixty years ago, a child had been found on the fell in a winter storm, wrapped
in a wolfskin and howling with the wind. He was brought to Wuthering Hall,
whose master, a childless widower, raised him alongside his own daughter. The
foundling grew fierce and brilliant and ungovernable, a storm in the shape of
a boy. He loved the daughter with the whole of his hungry nature, and the
daughter — who was, said Mrs. Dean, his equal in fierceness and his superior
in cruelty — loved him in return, which was the ruin of them both, for she
would not own it, and he would not own anything else.

When she married a man of property from the south, the foundling disappeared
into the moors for a year and returned rich, dressed in black, with a title
and a castle and a manner that made the servants cross themselves. He had gone,
the rumour ran, to the Continent, where he had learned the one trade that a
foundling can learn that will make the world give way before him: the trade
that never grows old. He did not age, after that, the way other men aged. He
married, and his wife died. He was seen at Carfax with his window lit at all
hours. He received guests, and the guests were not seen again.

"And the daughter," I said, "the one he loved?"

"Lived to old age," said Mrs. Dean, "and died sane, which was remarked upon as
a wonder, and left a granddaughter."

"Miss Linton."

The bread was punched down with finality. "Catherine Linton is the last of the
blood," she said. "And Lord Draven has waited sixty years for the blood to
thin to a single thread. He has a patience that the fell itself could learn
from. He is the fell. He is what lives in the houses men build to keep out the
fell, when they forget that the fell was here first."

"But Catherine says he writes to her — that her husband writes to her."

Mrs. Dean looked at me then with those eyes that had seen too much and decided
not to be impressed. "Her husband," she said, "was a good man, and is dead,
and stays dead, because that is the kind of good man he is. The letters come
from Carfax. They have always come from Carfax. They come at midnight because
the post at midnight is his post. And she answers them, Mr. Lockwood. Every
night, in her room, she writes back to the dead, and the dead reads her
letters, and that is how he has kept the thread from breaking all these
years."

"But that is —" I searched for a word that would not give offense. "That is
grief."

"That is grief," she agreed. "And grief is how the door is kept open. When you
love the dead, you are never quite in the living world, and the one who
dwells between the worlds knows your address, and your habits, and the hour at
which your lamp is lit." She wiped her hands on her apron and regarded the
dough with the satisfaction of a job finished. "He has waited sixty years. He
can wait the winter. And when the fell is frozen and the road is closed and
there is no help for ten miles in any direction, he will come up the fell road
in his black coach, and he will not knock, and he will be received."

"The doctor is gone," I said. "The creature is gone. There is only the two of
us and Miss Linton."

"There is always only the two of us," said Mrs. Dean. "There has been only the
two of us and the one between, since I was a girl. The difference now is that
you are here, Mr. Lockwood, and you are the first man of law to enter this
house in sixty years, and I do not know if that is an accident, or a will, or
a summons."

"The letter said he would correct a legal error."

"Ay," she said, and her voice had gone low and strange. "So it is a will. You
have come to read the will of a house, and the house has a different lawyer,
and his name is older, and he has already filed his claim." She looked at me
with the first expression I had seen on her face that was not resignation: it
was something closer to hope, and it frightened me more than the fear had.
"You have come at night, Mr. Lockwood, to a house that decides nothing after
dark. Do you know why that is?"

"Miss Linton told me. Decisions should not be taken in the dark."

"Because," said Mrs. Dean, "in the dark, the house gets a vote."

That night I sat in the parlour with the lamp burning high, and I wrote to Mr.
Prynne a letter that I did not send, telling him that I had contracted a
melancholy, and recommending a colleague for the matter, and describing the
route home in detail. And I did not send it, because when I had finished, I
read it, and I found that I had written, in the margin, in a hand that I did
not recognize as entirely my own, a single word.

*Stay.*

# IV. What Catherine Wrote

## Letters from the Desk of Catherine Linton

Mrs. Dean, in the course of her duties — and I have never been able to decide
whether it was duty or design — gave me a packet of letters tied with black
ribbon. They were addressed to Isabella, a school friend of Catherine's, and
were dated across the seven years of her widowhood. They had never been sent.
I record them here with the conviction that the reader will understand, as I
came to understand, that a woman who writes letters she does not send is a
woman speaking to someone who will not listen, which is the most honest
speaking there is.

> *Dear Isabella,*
>
> *You ask how I am, and I would tell you, but the telling would come out
> wrong, the way the light comes out wrong in this house, so that even at noon
> the rooms seem to be keeping something in reserve. I am the mistress of a
> house that was not built for me, full of things I did not choose: a lamp in
> a tower that I am afraid to look at and afraid to see extinguished; a doctor
> who makes, in his own defence, the most terrible things; and the letters. *
> *Always, the letters.*
>
> *I should not write to you. I write to you because you are the only one I
> ever told about him, and I am afraid that if I stop telling anyone, he will
> become true. Do you understand? As long as I say it is grief, it is a story
> with an end. The moment I stop saying it, it becomes a presence, and
> presences do not have ends.*
>
> *Yours, in the morning,*
> *C.*

> *Dear Isabella,*
>
> *Tonight I looked into the mirror in the east room — the tall one, in the
> silver frame, that my grandmother said was brought from France by a woman
> who married below her station and died above her deserts. I looked, and I
> was not there.*
>
> *I do not mean that I did not see myself. I mean that I saw the room, and
> the lamp, and the door ajar behind me, and the empty place where my
> reflection should have been, as if I had been removed from the world and the
> world had been told not to mention it. I put my hand to the glass. It was
> cold in the way that the moors are cold at midnight: cold with a purpose.
> And behind me, in the glass that would not show me, I saw — I cannot write
> this, and I will write it, because I will not let it be true by being
> unspoken — I saw the shape of a man standing in the doorway, and the shape
> was dressed in black, and the shape did not have a face, because in the
> mirror that had given up my reflection there was not enough room for two of
> us, and he had taken the room, and the face, and the name.*
>
> *I turned around. The doorway was empty.*
>
> *I have not been able to look at a glass since. Mrs. Dean has had the east
> room mirror covered, and she does not ask why, which is how I know that she
> knows, and that she has always known, and that the knowing is a
> housekeeping.*
>
> *Do not come to Wuthering Hall, Isabella. Whatever you hear of me, do not
> come. The house is a debt, and debts call in the principal, and the
> principal has a coach.*
>
> *C.*

> *Dear Isabella,*
>
> *You have heard, I am sure, the story of the foundling, the way one hears
> the story of a wolf: as a warning, and a wonder, and a thing that has been
> over by daylight for sixty years. Let me tell you the true story, the one
> Mrs. Dean will not tell because it is the one she has kept longest.*
>
> *He was not a foundling of the kind the story tells. He was a child of the
> moor, yes, found in a wolfskin, and taken in by my great-grandfather, who
> was a kind man with a terrible habit of collecting things that the world had
> rejected. He raised the boy as a son. The boy and my grandmother grew up
> together, and the boy loved her the way the wind loves the fell — not as a
> choice, but as a law of nature.*
>
> *And she loved him. I have my grandmother's journals, Isabella, and I have
> read what she wrote of him, and I know the grammar of it, because I have
> written the same sentences myself about a man who was not him. She loved him
> the way one loves the weather: in the blood, and against the weather's own
> intentions.*
>
> *But she married a gentleman, because the world was watching, and the world
> is the world. And the boy went into the moors, and he was gone a year, and
> when he came back he was not a boy, and he was not entirely, I think, a
> man. He had found, in the countries beyond the sea, the one thing that a
> man who has been rejected can make of himself: a creature of the dark, who
> is never refused again.*
>
> *He loved her to the end of her life. She is the reason he stayed near this
> fell at all, in his castle of Carfax, with his lamp and his coach and his
> visitors who were not seen again. And when she died, I was a girl, and he
> came to the funeral, and he stood at the back of the church in the black
> coat that had no age, and he looked at me — at me, Isabella — for a long
> time. And then he smiled, and went away, and I have known, from that day,
> that I was not a stranger to him, and that in his long ledger I had been
> written down as a debt that was coming due.*
>
> *My husband was the kindest man I ever knew. He knew about the smile. He
> knew about the ledger. He married me anyway, and he spent seven years*
> *teaching me that there are houses you can live in that the fell cannot
> reach. And when he died — of a winter illness, the doctor said, of a weak
> heart, the doctor said — he was buried in the chapel yard, and the night he
> was buried, the letters began.*
>
> *I have never told anyone that I answer them.*
>
> *I answer them because I am afraid that if I do not answer, he will come to
> the house to collect his debt in person, and I have seen what happens to
> debts that are collected in person. I answer them to keep him writing. A
> man who writes is a man who is waiting, and a man who is waiting is a man
> who can be waited out.*
>
> *But he has waited sixty years. He can wait the winter. And I am running
> out of letters, Isabella. I am running out of replies. I am running out of
> mornings when the mirror shows me where I stand.*
>

The packet ended there. I returned it to Mrs. Dean without comment, and she
took it without asking what I had read, and we stood in the kitchen of
Wuthering Hall as the winter dark came down, and I understood, at last, the
arithmetic of the house: a foundling and a debt and a bloodline that had been
thinning toward a single thread; a physician who had made a creature in the
image of his own loneliness; a woman who wrote letters to the dead to keep the
dead from calling in person. Three rooms of a house, each locked against the
others, and all of them locked against the night.

And the night, I was beginning to understand, had a key to all of them.

I sat long with the telegrams spread before me in the harbour office, while
the winter sea beat against the shingle below, and I understood that the
documents had done what documents always do: they had given the story a
sequence, and the sequence had given it a direction, and the direction was a
black coach climbing the fell road toward a house where a woman was writing
letters to the dead, and a lamp was burning in a tower, and a creature was
waiting in the dark with the patience of a creditor.

The winter had closed the road. The telegrams had stopped. And in the morning,
when I returned to Wuthering Hall, I found the front door open, and the
parlour lamp burning at noon, and Mrs. Dean standing in the hall with her
hands folded, wearing the expression of a woman who has kept a thing long
enough.

"He has come," she said.

"Who?"

"The one who does not knock." She stepped aside, and I saw the door of the
parlour standing open, and within it, seated in Catherine's chair, a figure
in black, with a wolf's fur collar at his throat, who rose as I entered with
the unhurried courtesy of a man who has all the time in the world, which, I
was beginning to understand, he very nearly did.

"Mr. Lockwood," said Lord Dorian Draven. "I have been expected. Welcome."

And I found, to my horror, that the words had been true before he spoke them:
that the house had known, and the fell had known, and the lamp had known, and
that I had been expected since the night I arrived, and that the expectation
was the only thing in the house that had ever been entirely certain.

# VII. The Winter of the Inquiries

## An Extract from the Grimhaven Mercury

I include here the only account of the matter that reached print, a paragraph
from the *Grimhaven Mercury*, for the second of January, which I have kept
against the day when the telling of the whole might be called for. It is the
newspaper's manner to be wrong, and it is the newspaper's manner to be wrong
in the right direction.

> *THE CARFAX PHENOMENON.* — We are informed, on excellent authority, that
> the extraordinary circumstances which have lately occupied the attention of
> this district are at length approaching a resolution. It is reported that
> Miss Catherine Linton of Wuthering Hall, the sole heiress of that ancient
> property, has received at her house during the present hard weather a
> gentleman of foreign education, Lord Dorian Draven of Carfax Castle, whose
> arrival by sea, in a vessel found masterless, has already been the subject

> singular affair has everywhere excited.

I quote this because it is necessary to record the shape of the world's
account before recording the shape of the truth, and because the two have
rarely, in the history of this fell, been observed to resemble one another.

## The Inquiry by Hypnosis

The evening of the twenty-second of December, Mrs. Dean — who had, I now know,
been waiting her whole tenure for someone to ask the question correctly —
produced from her stores a volume bound in a cloth of such faded violet that
it might have been the colour of memory itself. It was, she said, the journal
of her own mother, who had served the grandmother of the house, and it
contained, pressed between the leaves, the record of an inquiry conducted by
the physician of that generation into the nature of the foundling.

The method was not a method I would have sanctioned in daylight. It was the
method of the hypnotist: the patient was seated before the lamp, and the lamp
was the only light, and the physician spoke, and the patient answered, and
the answers were written down as they came, without mercy and without
correction. The patient was the grandmother of the house. The record, which I
transcribe, is written in a hand that trembles with the effort of keeping
pace.

The record broke off there. Mrs. Dean's mother had written, in the margin,
in a hand much older than the ink of the questions: *I have burned the
wolfskin. It grew back. I have burned it again. It grows in the attic, under
the boards, where the lamp does not reach. I have stopped burning it. It is
not a skin. It is a signature.*

I set the volume down. The lamp of the west tower, which we had left burning
as the doctor had asked, threw the shadow of the casement across the floor,
and I watched the shadow, and the shadow did not move with the flame, and I
understood that we had been keeping the lamp lit for reasons that had nothing
to do with the dark, and everything to do with the thing that the dark was
keeping.

"That is the whole of the inquiry," said Mrs. Dean. "It is the whole of what
we know. And the whole of what we know is that the foundling was not the
first, and not the last, and that the wolf is not the wolf, and the coach is
not the coach, and the debt is not money, and the winter is not weather."

"What is the winter, then?" I asked.

"The winter," said Mrs. Dean, "is the hour of the calling. The fell has kept
the house for sixty years, and the house has kept the lamp, and the lamp has
kept the doctor, and the doctor has kept the creature, and the creature has
kept the ink, and all of it has been keeping one thing: the thread. And the
thread is Catherine."

"And the master of Carfax?"

"Ay," she said. "He is the one the thread was written for. He is the
signature. He is the wolf, and the cargo, and the captain, and the debt, and
he has come to be read." She looked at me with those old eyes, and I saw, for
the first time, that they were the eyes of a woman who had spent forty years
keeping a house against a thing she had never once been asked to describe.
"He will ask her tonight, Mr. Lockwood. He will ask her to sign. And a
signature is a form, and a will is a form, and you are a clerk, and it is
your trade to know that the one who brings the pen wins the paper."

"Unless," I said, "the paper has another claimant."

She looked at me. And for the first time in all the days I had known her, the
iron of the Yorkshire voice broke, and there was something underneath it that
was almost — I will not say hope, for the word is too small — but almost
occupancy, as if a room in her that had been empty for forty years had been
found to have a tenant after all.

"There is one other claimant," she said. "He is patient. He is lonely. He is
waiting in the dark of the west tower, and the lamp is out, and he knows the
hour of the calling, because the hour was written into him by the same hand
that wrote the plans." She lowered her voice, though there was no one within
hearing. "The creature has never once asked for the blood. He has asked only
for what was promised to him. And the pen promised him a companion. And the
pen is the only law this house has ever kept."

That night, for the first time since my arrival, the wind fell silent over the
fell, and in the silence the house seemed to hold its breath, and from the
west tower, very faintly, as if from a great distance and a great patience, I
heard a sound that was not the wind, and was not the house, and was, I would
have sworn before any court that would hear me, the sound of a creature
beginning, at last, to be read.

# VIII. The Frozen Moor

## The Confrontation

I will record what I witnessed on the night of the twenty-fourth of December
as plainly as I am able, and the reader will believe as much of it as the
reader can, which I have learned is the manner in which the truth is
accepted in this district: in portions, like a debt.

The snow began at dusk and did not cease. By the hour of midnight the fell was
a single white silence, and the road was gone, and the sky was the colour of
milk that has stood too long, and the moon, when it rose, rose through a
screen of cloud that gave it the appearance of a lamp seen through a dirty
glass. The house was warm, which was wrong; Mrs. Dean had built up the fires
until they roared, and the parlour was bright, and Catherine sat in her chair
by the hearth in a gown the colour of winter itself, and opposite her, in
the chair that had been empty since the death of her husband, sat Lord
Draven.

I will not describe his face. I have discovered that the descriptions of him
that have been attempted — by the doctor, by the creature, by the hypnotic
record — all fail in the same place: they describe the age, and the eyes, and
the coat, and they do not describe the thing that was not there. He was not
entirely present. That is the whole of it. He sat in the chair of the living
with the courtesy of a man who has been patient so long that patience has
become his occupation, and he was attended, invisibly, by the attentiveness of
the dark.

"You have read the documents," he said. It was not a question.

"I have read the documents," I said. "They are not in order."

"All wills are in order, Mr. Lockwood, from the point of view of the one who
wrote them." He smiled. "The testator is the one who decides what is in
order. And I have been the testator of this house for sixty years."

"You are not the testator. You are the claimant."

"I am the claimant," he agreed, "and the claimant is the testator, when the
blood has thinned to a single thread. Catherine is the thread." He turned to
her, and for a moment the courtesy of his manner dropped, and I saw — I will
write this, because the record must be kept — I saw the boy in the wolfskin,
who had loved the house that would not have him, and who had gone into the
dark and been made into the thing that is never refused. "I have waited,"
he said to her, in a voice that was gentler than I could have borne, "sixty
years for the thread. I have written to you, and you have answered, and the
answers are the only letters I have ever received in which I believed a word
of the signature. You are the last of the blood, Catherine. And I am the last
of the patience. We are the two ends of the same thread, and the thread is
the will, and the will is the debt, and the debt is due tonight, on the
frozen fell, where the road is closed and no help can come and the calling is
answered."

"By whom?" said Catherine. Her voice was steady. It had been steady, I think,
for seven years, which is how long she had been practising. "The will is due,
you say. But the house has more than one signature, my lord. The house has a
doctor, who wrote in a hand that was not his own. The house has a creature,
who was promised a companion and was given a pen instead. And the house has a
clerk." She looked at me. "The clerk is the only one here whose trade is
words. And I have learned, my lord, from seven years of your letters, that
the one who brings the pen wins the paper."

Lord Draven smiled, and the smile was not kind, and it was not cruel; it was
the smile of a man who has been patient for sixty years and is about to be
patient no longer.

"The clerk," he said, "writes the letters I have not asked him to write, in a
margin that was not his, the word *stay*. The clerk has been expected, and
has known it, and has stayed. The clerk is the pen, Catherine, and the pen is
mine, and I have been writing with clerks since before this house was built."

I felt the room change then, in the manner that the fell changes when the
storm begins to gather its true force: not a movement, but a lowering, a
settling of the pressure. The fires roared. The lamp of the parlour bent
toward him, as if it would rather be dark. And from the doorway behind him —
from the dark of the passage that led to the west tower, where the lamp had
gone out — there came a voice, and the voice was deep, and it was patient,
and it was the voice of the creature.

"The pen is not yours," said the creature. "The pen was written by my maker,
and my maker was written by you, and you are the ink, and the ink has been
read. And the ink has one more debt that is older than the house, and the
debt is to me."

Lord Draven did not turn. He had not needed to turn since the door of the
house had opened to him. But I saw his hands, in the firelight, tighten upon
the arms of the chair, and I saw that for all his sixty years of patience, he
had not been expecting the creature, because the creature had never once in
all the record of the house asked for what was his.

"You," said Lord Draven, and there was in his voice, for the first time, the
note of a man who must attend to a matter of business. "The doctor's work. I
had not thought the doctor would finish you."

"The doctor did not finish me," said the creature. "The doctor was the pen,
and the pen was the plans, and the plans were yours, and you wrote the plans
to make a thing that would hold the house for you, a thing that would be
lonely enough to stay, and patient enough to wait, and you made me in the
image of your own patience, and the image was truer than the original." He
came into the room. The fire, I swear, leaned away from him. He was not what
the doctor's records had led me to expect; he was tall, and terrible, and
grief-stricken in the manner of a thing that has been given a shape by the
only hands it has ever known. "You wrote the plans to make a keeper of the
thread," he said. "You did not write that the keeper would learn to read."

The room was very still. Even the wind was still. Lord Draven rose from the
chair, and I understood, in the rising, that he was older than the house, and
older than the fell, and that he had been old before the wolfskin was found in
the storm, and that the boy in the wolfskin had been a vessel for something
that had been travelling toward this room for centuries, down the thread of
every house that had ever taken in a lost thing and been unable to be rid of
it.

"The keeper is not the claimant," he said. "The keeper is the debt's own
instrument. I wrote you to wait. You have waited. Now the waiting is over,
and the thread is called, and the debt is mine, and the creature returns to
the dark from which the doctor's pen was only ever a loan."

"No," said the creature.

It was the first refusal in the record of the house. Lord Draven stopped.

"No?" The word was soft. It was the softness of a man who has not been
refused in sixty years and is preparing himself for the novelty.

"No," said the creature. "You are the ink, and the ink has been read, and the
reading has shown that you are the copy. You wrote the plans to make me in
the image of your patience. But you did not write the plans, my lord. You
found the plans, as my maker found them, in a ship that was masterless, in a
hold that was full, in a log that ended with the words *the captain is on
board*. You are not the captain." He took a step into the room. "The captain
is the one who wrote the wolfskin, and the wolfskin was a signature, and the
signature was the seed, and the seed was planted in the house that would not
refuse a lost thing, and it has grown, through sixty years and three
generations of the blood, into the calling. The captain is coming. The
captain has come. The captain has been standing in this room since the night
a clerk was expected, and a clerk arrived, and stayed."

I do not know what came over me. I have thought about it for the rest of my
life, and I have never been able to account for it, and I will record it
without apology. The creature looked at me. And in the firelight, in the
parlour of Wuthering Hall, on the frozen fell, at the hour of the calling, I
heard myself speak, and the voice that spoke was mine, and it was not mine;
it was the voice of the one who had been written into me, as the plans had
been written into the doctor, and the ink into the creature, and the debt
into the house.

"Bring the pen," I said.

# IX. The End of the Matter

## The Signing

What followed was not a struggle. It was a signing. I understand now that the
whole of the affair, from the foundling in the wolfskin to the ship that
arrived masterless to the letters that were answered at midnight, had been
moving toward the table in the parlour of Wuthering Hall with the
directedness of a document moving toward its seal.

Mrs. Dean brought the ledger from the west tower. It was the Case-Log, the
elephant folio bound in leather black as dried blood, and it had been waiting
for this since the first entry. She set it upon the table. Lord Draven watched
it with the expression of a man who recognizes a signature he has signed a
great many times.

"The thread is the will," he said. "The will is the debt. The debt is due. And
the due is signed in blood, which is the only ink that the house has ever
kept."

"Then we shall sign," said the creature, "in the ink of the house." And he
came forward, and he took the pen from the table — the doctor's pen, the pen
that had written the plans and the ledger and the letters — and he laid his
hand upon the page of the ledger, and I saw, in the firelight, that the hand
was the hand of a thing that had been made, and that it had been made in the
image of a patience that was about to end.

"There is a pen," he said, "that is older than the doctor's, and older than
the plans, and older than the wolfskin." He held the doctor's pen up, and the
firelight passed through it strangely, and I understood that the pen was not
a pen at all, but a thing that had been shaped like one, a vessel, a holder,
a keeper of the ink that had been written into it by a hand that wrote in a
land of wolves and snow. "The pen was the seed," said the creature. "And the
seed has grown. And the seed is the ink. And the ink is me."

Lord Draven stood very still. I saw, for the first time in all the record of
the house, something like fear move across the face of the master of Carfax:
not the fear of a man who is losing, but the fear of a man who is being read,
who has been read all along, and who has only now discovered that the reader
was never going to stop at the signature.

"You are the ink," said Lord Draven slowly, tasting the words. "You are not
the instrument. You are the account itself."

"The account is the ink," said the creature, "and the ink does not forget,
and the ink has been written, and the writing is the will, and the will is
the debt, and the debt is this: that the one who wrote me is answered, and
that the answer is read, and that the reading is the end." He took the
doctor's pen, and he held it over the page of the ledger, and he looked at
Lord Draven, and he said, "The captain came by ship, and the ship was
masterless, and the captain was the cargo, and the cargo was the plans, and
the plans were the maker of the maker of me. You wrote me to wait for the
thread. But the thread is not Catherine." He turned, and he looked at
Catherine Linton, who had risen from her chair and stood with her hand upon
the back of it, and the firelight was in her grey eyes, and she was, for the
first time in seven years, not writing a letter. "The thread," said the
creature, "was always the one who would refuse the debt. And the refusal is
the signature, and the signature is made, and the making is here, and the
maker is the one who made a thing that could learn to read."

I understood, then, the arithmetic of the house, and it was not the arithmetic
of the doctor, or the foundling, or the wolf. It was the arithmetic of the
pen. The doctor had made a creature and had not finished him. The foundling
had made a house and had not been able to be rid of it. The master of Carfax
had made a plan and had not written the last page. And I — a clerk, whose
trade was words, who had been expected and had stayed — had been the one
thing none of them had written: the reader.

I took the ledger. I took the doctor's pen from the hand of the creature, who
gave it up without resistance, as if it had always been meant to end in the
hand of a clerk. And I wrote, at the foot of the last entry, in the margin
where the hand that was not mine had written *stay*:

> *The house is settled. The debt is paid. The will is closed. — G. Lockwood,
> clerk, for the estate of Wuthering Hall.*

And I wrote, beneath it, in the ink of the house, one further line, which I
did not plan and did not understand until I had finished, and which, I have
since decided, was the whole of the matter:

> *Whatever souls are made of, his and mine are the same.*

The pen moved. The fire burned. And in the ledger, the words of the house were
finally read.

## The Morning

I record the morning of the twenty-fifth of December, and it is the last
entry I will make in the record of the house, because the record is closed.

The snow had ceased. The fell lay white and silent under a sky the colour of
pewter, and the sun, when it rose, rose weak and without authority, as the sun
rises over this district in winter, as if it were a visitor uncertain of its
welcome. I walked out upon the fell in the dawn, and I stood where the road
had been, and I looked across the white silence toward the sea.

The castle of Carfax was gone. It had not burned; there was no smoke. It had
not fallen; there was no ruin. It was simply not there, in the manner that a
signature is not there after the page has been turned. The cliff where it had
stood for a century was bare to the wind. And on the shore below, where the
*Demeter* had grounded, there was no wreck, and no shingle, and no sea, only
the white of the fell running down to the white of the sky, and in the
distance, very far out, the white of something that might have been ice.

Mrs. Dean found me there as the light gathered. She stood beside me, and we
looked out together in the manner of two people who have been reading the
same document and have come, separately, to the last page.

"The creature," I said.

"Gone with the lamp," she said. "The tower is dark, and the bed is empty, and
the note is gone from the pillow. He has gone to the sea, Mr. Lockwood, to
the land of wolves and snow, where the captain was written, and where the
ink is finally kept. He was promised a companion, and I believe," she said,
"that he has gone to read her at last, and that she has been waiting, and
that the ice is not the cold that the records describe, but the place where
the promises of the pen are kept."

"And Lord Draven?"

Mrs. Dean was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was not the
iron voice, and not the soft voice, but a third thing that I had never heard
her use, as if the house, at last, had released a tenant it had been holding.

"The master of Carfax was not a man, and he was not a wolf, and he was not a
plan," she said. "He was a thing that had been written to be refused, and
that had been refused once, sixty years ago, by a house that took him in,
and that had spent sixty years making sure it would never be refused again.
And last night, on the frozen fell, at the hour of the calling, he was
refused by a creature who had learned to read, and the refusal was the
signature, and the signature was the end, and he has gone the way of the
cargo when the hold is empty." She looked at me. "He is not buried, Mr.
Lockwood. He is read. And the reading is over."

## The Last Leaf

I have settled the papers. The will is executed; the house is conveyed; the
title passes, in the ordinary manner, to Catherine Linton, who has signed
without hesitation, and who has asked, in the manner of a woman who has been
writing letters to the dead for seven years, that the ledger of the house be
given to me, as the reader of its final page.

I have written to Mr. Prynne that the melancholy of the district has been
much exaggerated, and that the client is well, and that the winter, though
severe, has not been remarkable.

I have not written to him the rest. I have not written that the west tower
lamp has gone out and that the house is warmer for it. I have not written
that Catherine and I keep the parlour fire high and do not speak of the
letters. I have not written that I walked out upon the fell this evening, at
dusk, and that I found, at the place where the road to Carfax used to begin,
a single footprint in the snow, and that the footprint was neither mine, nor
Catherine's, nor Mrs. Dean's, and that it was going, not toward the house,
but away from it, toward the sea.

I have not written that I followed it a little way, and that the way was
longer than it should have been, and that when I looked up the moor had gone
quiet, and the sky had gone the colour of the sea, and the snow beneath my
feet was not snow at all but a white that had no weather in it, and that
there, where the fell should have met the ice, there was no fell, and no ice,
only the promise.

And I have not written that a voice spoke, very softly, out of the white, and
that the voice was patient, and that it was the voice of the creature, and
that it said:

*"You have read us, clerk. Now you must keep us. The ink does not forget, and
the ink has chosen you. Whatever souls are made of, the reading is the
keeping, and the keeping is the end, and the end is a page that is never
turned."*

I closed the ledger. I came back to the house along the fell road, and the
road was a road again, and the dusk was a dusk, and the lamp of the parlour
burned steady in the window, and Catherine stood in the doorway, and she did
not ask where I had been, and I did not tell her, and we stood together
looking out at the winter as the dark came down, and I understood at last
that Wuthering Hall had never been a house at all.

It was a book. And I had been expected, and I had stayed, and the reading,
which is the keeping, had begun.

*Here ends the record of Wuthering Hall, as delivered by Mr. Gabriel Lockwood,
clerk, in the winter of 1888, and here ends the debt of the house, which is
to say that the debt is paid, and the ink is kept, and the page is not
turned.*

# Appendix B. The Questions of the Pen

For the scholar who has followed the record to its last page, a few notes on
the text, in the manner of an edition.

- The epigraph that opens this record — *whatever souls are made of, his and
  mine are the same* — occurs twice: once above the title, and once in the
  margin of the signing. It is the only sentence in the whole of the record
  that is not, so far as can be determined, the composition of any of the
  parties. It was written into the margin, and it reads as if it had been
  waiting to be written.
- The creature is never named in the body of the text; the name "Adam"
  appears only in the note he left for the doctor, and in the case-log
  index, where it stands in a hand that the doctor himself does not use
  elsewhere.
- The doctor's notebook is the only portion of the record set in the format
  of the machine that produced this edition. The editor has preserved the
  format on the grounds that the format is part of the evidence: the leaves
  were written to be read by a reader, and the reader was expected.
- No record of a ship called the *Demeter* is to be found in the harbour
  ledgers of Whitby or Grimhaven after the winter of 1888. The telegrams,
  likewise, have no file. The editor has preserved them on the authority of
  the single surviving witness, whose account is delivered in the manner of
  a man who has been expected and has stayed.
- The final question — whether the creature found his companion on the ice,
  whether the captain was ever read, whether the page is ever turned — is
  not answered in the record. The editor has determined that the question is
  not the property of the editor, and that the reading, which is the keeping,
  is best left to the reader.

*Here the record, and the winter, are closed.*
